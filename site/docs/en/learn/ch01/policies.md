# Policies

The transition model answers what the environment does after an action. A **policy** answers the question that comes just before it: given the current state, which action will the agent request?

A policy belongs to the agent. It can be written by a person, generated by an algorithm, or learned from experience. It does not have to be optimal or learned to count as a policy.

<a id="learning-goals"></a>

## Learning goals

After this unit, you should be able to:

1. read and validate a policy row $\pi(\cdot\mid s)$;
2. distinguish deterministic and stochastic policies;
3. explain why a policy is neither one action nor one trajectory; and
4. separate randomness in action selection from randomness in the environment.

<a id="policy-as-distribution"></a>

## A policy is a distribution over available actions

For a state $s$, a policy assigns a probability to every available action:

$$
\pi(a\mid s)
=
\Pr(A_t=a\mid S_t=s).
$$

Each policy row must be a probability distribution:

$$
\pi(a\mid s)\ge 0,
\qquad
\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\pi(a\mid s)=1.
$$

The notation $\pi(\cdot\mid s)$ means the entire row of action probabilities at state $s$. A single sampled action is only one outcome from that row.

In this chapter we use a **stationary Markov policy**: the row depends on the current state, not directly on the time step or the complete history. A complete policy provides a row for every state where a decision can be made. If a goal is terminal, the environment stops before the policy is queried there.

<a id="deterministic-and-stochastic"></a>

## Deterministic and stochastic policies

A deterministic policy selects one action with probability 1. It can be represented by a function $\mu(s)$:

$$
\pi(a\mid s)=
\begin{cases}
1, & a=\mu(s),\\
0, & a\ne\mu(s).
\end{cases}
$$

A stochastic policy gives positive probability to more than one action. For example, consider state $s_{10}$ in the original 4×4 lab:

| Action $a$ | Up | Right | Down | Left | Stay |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\pi(a\mid s_{10})$ | 0 | 0.65 | 0.30 | 0 | 0.05 |

The row is valid because every entry is nonnegative and the entries sum to 1. On one visit to $s_{10}$, the agent still requests exactly one action. The percentages describe frequencies over repeated samples, not fractions of a movement.

Randomness is not a synonym for poor decision-making. A stochastic policy can preserve exploration, avoid being predictable, or represent genuine uncertainty between useful choices.

<a id="gridworld-policy"></a>

## An original GridWorld policy

Define a deterministic “right-edge” policy for the 4×4 lab:

- request **right** while the current cell is not in the rightmost column;
- request **down** after reaching the rightmost column; and
- stop when the terminal goal $s_{15}$ is reached.

With no wind and start state $s_0$, this rule produces

$$
s_0\rightarrow s_1\rightarrow s_2\rightarrow s_3
\rightarrow s_7\rightarrow s_{11}\rightarrow s_{15}.
$$

The displayed sequence is a trajectory, not the policy itself. The policy is the rule that says what to request at every encountered state. A different start state or a windy transition can produce a different trajectory while the policy remains unchanged.

<a id="policy-versus-environment"></a>

## Two independent sources of randomness

The policy chooses a requested action through $\pi(a\mid s)$. The environment then chooses a next state through $p(s'\mid s,a)$. Combining them gives the one-step state distribution under the policy:

$$
p_\pi(s'\mid s)
=
\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}
\pi(a\mid s)\,p(s'\mid s,a).
$$

This equation keeps the responsibilities visible:

| Policy | Environment | What can vary? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deterministic | Deterministic | The trajectory is fixed once the start state is fixed |
| Stochastic | Deterministic | Requested action |
| Deterministic | Stochastic | Outcome of the requested action |
| Stochastic | Stochastic | Both action selection and action outcome |

In the current lab, you press the requested-action buttons yourself. Wind changes the actual action inside the environment. Therefore, varying outcomes under 40% wind do not prove that your decision rule is stochastic.

<a id="common-confusions"></a>

## Common confusions

- **Policy versus action:** a policy is a rule over states; an action is one decision produced by that rule.
- **Policy versus trajectory:** a policy can generate many trajectories because starts and transitions can differ.
- **Policy versus transition:** $\pi(a\mid s)$ belongs to the agent; $p(s'\mid s,a)$ belongs to the environment.
- **Probability versus partial motion:** a 65% chance of moving right means one right request is sampled on about 65% of repeated visits.
- **Stochastic versus arbitrary:** probabilities must still be deliberate, nonnegative, and normalized.

<a id="experiment-connection"></a>

## Experiment connection

Open the [Grid World concept lab](/en/labs/ch01-gridworld) and use the buttons to enact the right-edge policy.

1. Set wind to 0%, apply the configuration, and follow the state-based rule.
2. Check that requested and actual actions agree.
3. Set wind to 40%, reset, and apply the same rule at every visited state.
4. Observe cases where the rule still requests the same action for a state but the environment executes another action.
5. Repeat with the same seed, then with a different seed.

This isolates a deterministic policy from stochastic environment dynamics. The current experiment does not learn or optimize a policy.

<a id="check-understanding"></a>

## Check your understanding

1. Is a row with probabilities $0.5$, $0.2$, and $0.4$ a valid policy row? Why?
2. At $s_{10}$, suppose a policy requests right with probability $0.75$ and down with probability $0.25$. With deterministic motion, what are the probabilities of reaching $s_{11}$ and $s_{14}$?
3. A robot always requests right at state $s$, but repeated visits sometimes end in different next states. Must its policy be stochastic?

::: details Answers
1. No. The entries sum to $1.1$, so they are not a probability distribution.
2. $p_\pi(s_{11}\mid s_{10})=0.75$ and $p_\pi(s_{14}\mid s_{10})=0.25$.
3. No. The policy can be deterministic while the environment transition is stochastic.
:::
